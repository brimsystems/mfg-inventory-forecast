Section 1

Executive Summary

The shop buys about $5.7M of purchased material a year across 800 active items. This report looks at where those dollars sit, what demand patterns the shop is buying against, how supplier lead times are actually performing, and what a forecast-driven reorder policy would release in working capital. The window is September 2023 to August 2026.

Three findings drive the rest of the report. First, spending is highly concentrated: the top 169 items (21% of the catalog) account for 80% of annual consumption value, so a small slice of the book carries the working capital. Second, one supplier, Abbott-Munoz, has let its actual lead time drift from 12 days on file to about 23 days, and the stale figure quietly undersizes reorder points on roughly 125 items. Third, correcting the lead times and forecasting demand lifts the fill rate from 86% to 96% while holding inventory close to today's level, and at an equal service level it releases about $528K of working capital against a corrected-lead policy that simply buys more safety stock.

Section 2

Where the Inventory Dollars Sit

Before any forecasting, the first question for a plant manager is where the money is tied up. The answer is a textbook Pareto: a small number of high-value items dominate consumption, and the long tail of low-value parts barely moves the working-capital needle. That shape decides where tighter planning is worth the effort and where a simple rule is fine.

The practical read is that supplier and lead-time risk is diversified across several commodity groups rather than sitting behind one vendor relationship.

Section 3

Demand Pattern Distribution

Not every item should be planned the same way, because they do not sell the same way. Each part is classified by how regularly it is consumed and how variable the quantities are, into four patterns: smooth, erratic, lumpy, and intermittent. The chart contrasts each segment's share of the catalog with its share of the dollars, and the gap between the two bars is the point: the erratic and lumpy items are a minority of parts but the majority of the money, so that is where a demand forecast, rather than a static reorder point, pays off.

Demand Segments: Share of Items vs Share of Value

The erratic and lumpy segments together are 65% of items but 75% of the value. These are the parts that order regularly in volatile sizes, or arrive in sporadic large lots, and a reorder point tuned to average demand either stocks out or overstocks them. The smooth items plan cleanly with statistical reorder points, and the intermittent tail is best left on a simple min/max rule. The table below states the implication for each pattern.

Section 4

Supplier Lead-Time Performance

Reorder points are only as good as the lead time behind them, so the next check is whether the lead times on file still match what suppliers actually deliver. Comparing the figure stored in the ERP against the lead time now observed at receiving, almost every supplier sits on the diagonal: the actual lead time matches the file within about 2 day. One supplier is the exception. Abbott-Munoz is delivering in roughly 23 days against a file value of 12 days, a gap of 11 days that has crept up unnoticed.

One measurement caveat shapes how these lead times are read. Receiving tends to post several deliveries together in end-of-week batches, so the receipt date on file lands a little after the goods actually arrived. Left uncorrected that batching stretches the apparent order-to-receipt gap and biases the raw lead time upward by about two days. The figures here use a robust median across each supplier's receipts rather than a mean, so a handful of batched postings does not pull the number, and the drift shown below is a real change in supplier performance rather than an artifact of how receipts are keyed.

Lead Time on File vs Actual Lead Time by Supplier

The drift matters because the stale 12-day figure feeds the reorder-point formula on about 125 items bought from this vendor, so those items are reordered too late for how the supplier actually ships today. The estimated cost of that single stale number is roughly 312 stockouts a year and about $78K in expedite premiums to recover them. Correcting one lead time in the item master removes most of that exposure, and it is the first of the two levers the policy comparison below pulls.

Section 5

The Three-Policy Comparison

This is the payoff. The same 800 items were replayed over a twelve-month holdout under three replenishment policies: today's reorder points as they stand, the same points recomputed with the corrected lead times, and a forecast-driven policy that sizes each item from its demand forecast. Fill rate is priced with expedite events at $250 each and inventory carried at 22% a year. The comparison below holds service roughly constant so the inventory figures are read like for like.

Fill Rate and Inventory Value Under Each Policy

Reading the panels together tells the story. Today's policy runs a 86% fill rate on $902K of inventory, and pays $298K a year in expedite premiums to cover the gaps. Simply correcting the lead times lifts fill to 94%, but it gets there by buying safety stock: inventory climbs to $1.9M. The forecast-driven policy reaches essentially the same service, 96%, on just $1.4M, because it holds stock where demand variability actually warrants it rather than padding every item.

The headline is the working-capital release. Held at the same roughly 96% service level, the forecast-driven policy carries about $528K less inventory than the corrected-lead policy, a 28% reduction, worth roughly $116K a year in carrying cost alone. Against today's policy it also lifts fill from 86% to 96% and cuts expedite premiums from $298K to $119K, a saving of $180K. In short, better information, corrected lead times plus a demand forecast, buys higher service and lower working capital at the same time, rather than trading one for the other.

Section 5.1

Phantom On-Order and Stockouts

Part of today's stockout count is self-inflicted, and it is fixable without any forecasting. The ledger still carries 816 never-closed purchase order lines against 493 items, quantities the ERP counts as inbound even though the receipt never posted and never will. Because the on-order position looks covered, the reorder logic sees no shortfall and holds off buying. About 123 stockout events a year, roughly 10% of the 1,194 under today's policy, trace back to this phantom on-order rather than to a genuine demand surprise.

Current Stockouts Attributable to Phantom On-Order

The read for the buying team is that closing the dead POs is a clerical cleanup, not a planning project, yet it recovers about a tenth of the stockouts on its own. It also compounds with the policy changes above: the fill-rate gains from corrected lead times and a demand forecast assume the on-order position the system reports is real, so purging the phantom lines is a prerequisite for the reorder logic to act on the shortfalls it is meant to catch.
