Section 1

Results

Over ten weeks we audited the shop's ERP end to end, tested it for sixteen kinds of data quality error, and corrected what the record could support: 689 dead item records deactivated, 977 lead times and 874 reorder points recomputed from actual history, 145 duplicate records merged, 64 missing components restored to the bills of 30 products, 1,228 phantom purchase order lines closed, and 48 unit-of-measure conversions added. Seven ERP controls were switched on so the errors cannot re-enter, and a cycle-count program replaced the annual physical. The largest achievements are that the balances the shop plans against can now be trusted ($701,055 of inventory, 27% of the book value, at a reliable balance, from nothing), that $436,711 of on-order value that was never coming is off the books, and that the purchasing manager's 120 line-critical components are managed in the ERP instead of a spreadsheet. Section 1.1 gives one measure per control; Section 1.2 gives what the errors cost in 2025.

Section 2

Data Quality Errors

This data quality audit examined the shop's entire ERP system end to end. The system consists of three master-level tables (Item, Supplier and Bill of Materials) and five transaction-level tables (Inventory Ledger, Purchase Orders, Service Orders, Production Orders and Cycle Counts), related as shown below.

ERP Tables

Over the past 36 months, over 229K individual records were produced across the eight ERP tables. This audit covered all of them. It found 16 different types of data quality error recur over this period. These errors touched a significant share of the ERP's records and left it unable to serve as the trusted central record of what the shop holds, buys and builds. They also directly disrupted operational workflows, inflated costs and distorted financial visibility, as detailed below.

The tables below lay out the 16 different data quality error types that were found to recur over the three-year audit period.

Three findings stand out for their scale. The item master was full of dead records: 40% of its records (980 of 2,437) were items with no movement in two years or more that are still flagged active, and 319 of them still carry a reorder point. The parameters the ERP reorders on are stale: the lead times on 67% of live items no longer match how long deliveries take, running 7 days shorter than actual on average, and the reorder points on 60% of live items differ from what current usage and lead times call for by a median of 31 units, 44% of them set too low and the rest too high. And the majority of receipts are posted in batches: 57% of purchase order lines carry a posting date 3 days on average after the material arrived, so every lead time computed from receipt dates reads 3 days longer than the delivery took, every reorder point set from it carries 3 days of stock it does not need, and every supplier measured on it looks slower than it is.

Two other master-level errors are small in the master and large downstream. The 64 components missing from the bills of 30 of the 80 products are consumed on every job that builds those products without ever being subtracted, which makes them the single largest source of shortages and write-offs in the ledger. And the 241 duplicate records, each reordering on its own point against one shared pile, produced 258 purchase lines in 2025 for parts the shop already held.

A further pattern distorts the financial view of the company: the ledger is corrected after the fact rather than kept right. Material leaves the stockroom without a transaction (#9), and the gap that opens between the books and the shelf is closed later by an adjustment carrying no reason code (#13), the same way damage, receiving errors and count errors are closed. The balance therefore reads high until someone corrects it, and the correction says nothing about why. The 4,853 chronic write-offs under #9 are part of the 10,100 unexplained adjustments under #13: one is the leak, the other is why the leak went unnoticed.

Section 3

Error Remediation

Remediation ran in a fixed order. We profiled every table first (row counts by year, fill rates by column, the values in each code field, the date ranges), then ran one rerunnable test for each of the sixteen error types. Where the ERP could not settle a finding on its own we brought in evidence from outside it: the cycle counts against the book balances, the purchasing manager's spreadsheet against the ERP, and the consumption expected from jobs and bills of materials against what was actually issued. Findings that rested on judgment, such as duplicate pairs, free-text matches, dead-item dispositions and BOM additions, went to the people who own them for confirmation before anything was changed, and every correction was recorded in a reference table rather than written over the source, so each one is auditable and reversible.

The tables below give, for each error, how it was remediated and whose input that took, the evidence it rested on, and how many of the affected rows were remediated.

Section 4

Go-Forward Changes

The sixteen errors trace back to two root conditions rather than sixteen separate causes: the ERP allowed them, and nobody owned the routine upkeep that would have caught them. The corrections in Section 3 fix what those two conditions produced; the changes in this section stop them producing it again, and they fall into two groups that are different kinds of work. The first group are settings. Each was changed by the ERP administrator during the engagement, took effect for every user at once, and stops the error at the point of entry. This is the easier lift: the changes are already in place, they hold on their own, and they need nothing further from the shop. The table gives what each one does and which errors it closes (the error can no longer occur) or addresses (the error is caught or reduced, but not prevented).

The second group are habits. No setting can make someone count a shelf, review a bill of materials, close a purchase order or refresh a reorder point, so each of these needs a named owner and a cadence, and each will lapse without them. That makes this the harder lift: it depends on organizational alignment and on the owners' buy-in rather than on a configuration screen. The shop has committed to the owners and cadences below, and keeping them is what protects the results in Section 1.

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 1% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
  • What the cleanup is worth going forward is not measured here. This report claims only costs that trace to a specific error in the 2025 record. How much of the untraced rush spend and how many of the shortages clean data would have prevented is a forecast, and it belongs to the reorder-policy work that follows this audit.
