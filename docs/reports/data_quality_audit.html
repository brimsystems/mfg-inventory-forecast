Section 1

Results

Over ten weeks we audited the purchasing item master and the inventory ledger, corrected what could be corrected, and changed the ERP settings that let the problems recur. This section is the outcome, in the terms the shop runs on: what the errors cost in 2025, the last full year, and what the same year looks like when the same reorder rule runs on the corrected records. Every figure is labeled measured (read from the records), simulated (from a replay of 2025 on the corrected records) or estimated (resting on an assumption, stated where it is used). The sections that follow show what we found, what we did, and what still needs an owner.

The money is in the expedites and the line stops, not in the stock. In 2025 the shop paid $163,643 in rush freight and price premiums on 536 rush lines; $53,505 of that sits on lines whose cause is in the record (a reorder placed on a stale lead time, a pull that was never recorded, an order held back for material that was never coming), and the rest were demand spikes the stale reorder points did not cover. Those shortages delayed 401 of 2,155 jobs, typically by 7 days and by 4,686 days in total. Replaying the year with the same reorder rule on the corrected records, rush spend falls to $3,627, the shortage episodes to 16 and the delayed jobs to 6. The cleanup does not release much cash from inventory, and it would be wrong to claim it does: the corrected replay holds $2,697,932 of stock on average against $2,696,281 as recorded. $550,465 of excess disappears, almost all of it duplicate records that each reordered against one shared pile, but $552,116 of shortfall is filled on items whose stale reorder points and lead times had been running them short, and the net, $1,651 more held, is inside the noise of the simulation.

Inventory book value by balance reliability, before and after

Before the cleanup, only 25% of live items had a book balance we would trust to reorder against; 30% were unreliable and held 35% of the book value. After the cycle-count program and the master fixes, 62% are reliable and the unreliable share is down to 12%, each with a stated reason.

One item the buyer knows. A high-volume gearmotor was carried under two part numbers, each with its own reorder point, and its supplier's real lead time had crept from about two weeks to over a month while the ERP still read two weeks. The result was recurring line stops and air freight. Merged to one record, with the lead time recomputed from receipts and the reorder point recomputed from usage over it, the item now reorders early enough to cover the true lead time, and the expedites on it stop.
Section 2

Findings

This audit examined the shop's entire ERP system end to end. The system consists of three master-level tables (Item, Supplier and Bill of Materials) and five transaction-level tables (Inventory Ledger, Purchase Orders, Service Orders, Production Orders and Cycle Counts), related as shown in the entity relationship diagram below.

The ERP tables examined and the keys that join them

The audit covered over 225K individual records across the eight tables over the past 36 months, plus the purchasing manager's spreadsheet of the 120 components she tracks outside the system. It found 16 different data quality error types recurring over this period.

Section 2.2

Costs

Operationally, the errors show up as line stops, expedites and rework of the books. In 2025 the shop placed 536 rush orders, ran short 598 times on 406 items, and 401 of its 2,155 jobs waited for material, typically for 7 days. The purchasing manager keeps 120 components in a spreadsheet because the system's numbers cannot be trusted, 1,368 purchase order lines older than 90 days sit open with $583,620 on order that will never arrive, and the counts posted 4,994 balance corrections in the year. Replaying 2025 with the same reorder rule on the corrected records removes nearly all of it: 16 shortages, 6 delayed jobs and $3,627 of rush spend.

Financially, the errors touch four kinds of money, kept apart here because they do not add. Cash incurred: $163,643 of rush freight and price premiums in 2025, measured from the rush lines, of which $53,505 sits on lines with a recorded cause; this is the recurring saving once reorders fire on time. Working capital: the corrected replay holds about the same inventory as recorded ($2,697,932 against $2,696,281), because $550,465 of excess, almost all of it on duplicate records, is offset by $552,116 of shortfall on items the stale parameters were running short; the net cash release is nil. Misstatement: $789,331 was written off and $898,199 written up at counts in 2025, $49,327 of component cost was consumed on 30 products and never charged to them, and $583,620 of on-order value was fiction; the books were corrected, no cash returns, though the understated product costs call for a pricing review. Exposure: $105,926 of purchase suggestions sit on dead items (none were acted on), $1,217,770 of vendor spend is held under alias records and $215,419 was bought on free-text lines, all invisible to the reports that would use them. Three assumptions underlie these figures: quantities are valued at the item's standard cost, with the class median where the cost is blank; the corrected figures are a simulation of 2025 with the same reorder rule on the corrected masters, not a forecast; and shortages and delays are counted, not costed, because what a late job costs depends on the customer. Section 3

Error Remediation

Remediation ran in a fixed order. We profiled every table first (row counts by year, fill rates by column, the values in each code field, the date ranges), then ran one rerunnable test for each of the sixteen error types. Where the ERP could not settle a finding on its own we brought in evidence from outside it: the cycle counts against the book balances, the purchasing manager's spreadsheet against the ERP, and the consumption expected from jobs and bills of materials against what was actually issued. Findings that rested on judgment, such as duplicate pairs, free-text matches, dead-item dispositions and BOM additions, went to the people who own them for confirmation before anything was changed, and every correction was recorded in a reference table rather than written over the source, so each one is auditable and reversible.

Section 4

Go-Forward Changes

The sixteen errors trace back to two root conditions rather than sixteen separate causes: the ERP allowed them, and nobody owned the routine upkeep that would have caught them. The corrections in Section 3 fix what those two conditions produced; the changes in this section stop them producing it again, and they fall into two groups that are different kinds of work. The first group are settings. Each was changed by the ERP administrator during the engagement, took effect for every user at once, and stops the error at the point of entry. This is the easier lift: the changes are already in place, they hold on their own, and they need nothing further from the shop. The table gives what each one does and which errors it closes (the error can no longer occur) or addresses (the error is caught or reduced, but not prevented).

The second group are habits. No setting can make someone count a shelf, review a bill of materials, close a purchase order or refresh a reorder point, so each of these needs a named owner and a cadence, and each will lapse without them. That makes this the harder lift: it depends on organizational alignment and on the owners' buy-in rather than on a configuration screen. The shop has committed to the owners and cadences below, and keeping them is what protects the results in Section 1.

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 12% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
  • The corrected-year figures are a simulation. The 2025 replay on corrected records holds demand, suppliers and the reorder rule fixed and changes only the data the rule saw. It does not model the buyers learning the new numbers or a customer's reaction to a late job, so the expedite, shortage and inventory figures under "corrected" are the size of the effect, not a forecast of next year.
