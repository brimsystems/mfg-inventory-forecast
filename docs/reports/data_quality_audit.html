One item the buyer knows. A high-volume gearmotor was carried under two part numbers, each with its own reorder point, and its supplier's real lead time had crept from about two weeks to over a month while the ERP still read two weeks. The result was recurring line stops and air freight. Section 2

What we found

This audit examined one company's ERP system end to end. On the master side, the records that define what the shop buys and builds: the Item master (2,437 records), Bill of materials (747 records), Supplier master (44 records). On the transaction side, the history those masters govern: the Inventory ledger (183,255), Purchase order lines (29,746), Service order lines (9,874), Production orders (7,117), Cycle counts (4,056). That is 3 master-level components holding 3,228 records and 5 transaction-level components holding 234,048 records: 237,396 records in all across 36 months, plus the purchasing manager's spreadsheet of the 120 line-stopping components she tracks outside the system.

Two of the counts are rows that should exist rather than rows that do. For unrecorded consumption the affected rows are the write-off adjustments that stand in for the issues that were never entered; for BOM omissions they are the component rows missing from the bill of materials, counted against the complete bill.

Of the 94 items with three or more downward adjustments in the last year, 68% are BOM-omitted components: the adjustments are the shop absorbing usage the BOM never recorded. That overlap is the strongest single piece of evidence that the phantom inventory and the BOM gaps are the same problem.

Section 3

What we did

How the tests were run. The work ran in a fixed order. Before looking for anything specific, we profiled every component of the ERP plainly: row counts by year, the fill rate of every column, the distinct values in every code field, and the date ranges. That pass is what surfaced the blank cost and supplier fields and the MISC item class before any test was written, and it set the baseline every later comparison is measured against. Only then did we run one test per error type, the sixteen in the tables below. Every test was written as a query against the extracted tables, and the query and its output were kept, so each finding traces to a stated rule and the shop can rerun it later.

Where precision has a floor. Two tests were built to be robust to the errors they sit on top of. Actual lead times were computed from receipt history using the median and a trimmed 80th percentile rather than the mean, because receipts are batched to Mondays and month-end, and that posting lag puts a floor of a few days on how precisely any lead time can be known. Quantity errors were found as per-item outliers against each item's own median and spread rather than against averages, so a genuinely lumpy item is not flagged for being lumpy. Duplicate detection normalized descriptions (case, punctuation, fraction and decimal forms, unit tokens), compared only within an item class, and scored on description similarity, cost proximity and shared supplier, with a sample of candidate pairs checked by hand.

Nothing in the source data was overwritten by any of this. Every finding, every review decision and every correction was recorded in a reference table (the dead-item dispositions, the duplicate and supplier crosswalks, the UOM conversions, the lead-time computations, the chronic-adjustment list, the BOM change log, the document closures, the spreadsheet reconciliation, the free-text attribution and the posting corrections), so each one is auditable and reversible. The tables below give, for each error, how it was remediated and whose input that took, the evidence it rested on, and how many of the affected rows were remediated.

Section 5

What it means for purchasing

Clean data is only worth the decisions it changes. The corrected consumption history, merged records and recomputed lead times feed the reorder queue and the demand forecast. The three-way test below holds the model, features and horizons fixed and changes only how clean the input history is, so it isolates what the cleanup is worth.

Forecast error by cleaning tier (WAPE vs true demand over lead time)

Merging duplicate records (raw → master) cuts error by 8% overall and far more on the merged items themselves; adding back the unrecorded transaction usage (master → fully) cuts it a further 6%. The forecast-driven policy then converts that accuracy into service and dollars.

Inventory policy: current vs corrected lead times vs forecast-driven

At an equal, better service level the forecast-driven policy holds $1,543,774 of inventory against $2,302,270 for a simple corrected-lead-time policy, releasing $758,496 of working capital, while lifting fill from 89% and cutting stockouts from 2,071 to 632. About 21% of the old stockouts trace to phantom on-order: material the ERP believed was inbound on never-closed POs.

Keeping it clean

The corrections are worth nothing if the same problems return. Some fixes were made in the system during the engagement; the rest need an owner and a cadence.

Requires process and ownership (proposed).

  • A named item-master owner and a part-creation approval step, so no one can create a duplicate unchecked.
  • The cycle-count program continued on the ABC schedule, not allowed to lapse back to an annual count.
  • A monthly parameter refresh that recomputes reorder points from the forecast.
  • A monthly open-document review and a quarterly dead-item review.
  • A BOM review for every new product and option, so backflush stays complete.
  • The data-quality measures in this report tracked monthly against their targets.
Section 7

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 12% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
Section 8

Appendix

The audit ran one detection test per error type against the full ledger and item master, and produced a reference table for each remediation activity (dead-item dispositions, duplicate and supplier crosswalks, UOM conversions, lead-time computations, parameter recommendations, the chronic-adjustment list, the BOM change log, open-document closures, the spreadsheet reconciliation, the free-text attribution, and the posting corrections). Dollar figures for working capital, expedites and carrying cost are estimates on stated assumptions: an expedite fee of $250 per event and a 22% annual carrying rate, with service held constant when comparing inventory levels. Error types and rates reflect patterns commonly documented in manufacturing ERP systems.

How to read the numbers. Data-quality burden is reported three ways: the count of errorive records, the far larger count of transactions that reference a errorive master record (the blast radius), and the operational impact in unrecorded consumption, unreliable dollars, stockouts and working capital. This report leads with impact; the counts are in the tables above.
