Section 1

Results

Over ten weeks we audited the purchasing item master and the inventory ledger, corrected what could be corrected, and changed the ERP settings that let the problems recur. This section is the outcome, in the terms the shop runs on. Every dollar figure in it is measured from the 2025 record and traceable to a specific error; where the record does not support that attribution, no share is claimed. What clean data is worth going forward is a forecast rather than a measurement, and it is left to the reorder-policy work that follows this audit. The sections that follow show what we found, what we did, and what still needs an owner.

The money is in the expedites and the stopped lines, not in a stock reduction. In 2025 the shop placed 536 rush orders costing $163,643 in freight and price premiums. 206 of them, $53,505, were placed in one of three situations the errors create: a delivery running past a promised date that a stale lead time had set (180), a shelf emptied by a pull that was never recorded (17), or a reorder held back by an on-order balance that was never coming (9). The other 330 rush lines are not attributed to the errors and are not claimed. The same three situations account for 555 of the 598 shortage episodes in the year, which held 276 jobs at the moment they ran short. Inventory tells a different story: the errors did not so much inflate the stock as put it in the wrong places. 228 purchase lines worth $1,117,281 were placed on one item number while its duplicate record already held the quantity ordered, and $583,620 sat on order against lines that would never arrive, holding real reorders back. The cleanup returns none of that as cash. What it changes is that the next purchase, and the next count, are made against a number that is right.

Before the cleanup, only 25% of live items had a book balance we would trust to reorder against; 30% were unreliable and held 35% of the book value. After the cycle-count program and the master fixes, 62% are reliable and the unreliable share is down to 12%, each with a stated reason.

One item the buyer knows. A high-volume gearmotor was carried under two part numbers, each with its own reorder point, and its supplier's real lead time had crept from about two weeks to over a month while the ERP still read two weeks. Section 2

Findings

This data quality audit examined the shop's entire ERP system end to end. The system consists of three master-level tables (Item, Supplier and Bill of Materials) and five transaction-level tables (Inventory Ledger, Purchase Orders, Service Orders, Production Orders and Cycle Counts), related as shown below.

ERP Tables

Over the past 36 months, over 225K individual records were produced across the eight ERP tables. This audit covered all of them. It found 16 different types of data quality error recurring over this period. Taken together, these errors inflated costs, disrupted operational workflows, and distorted financial visibility.

Section 2.1

Data Quality Errors

The tables below present the 16 different data quality error types that were found to recur over the three-year audit period, with a description of each, the ERP table in which it was found, and its prevalence within that table. Three findings stand out for their scale. The item master is silted up: 40% of its records (980 of 2,437) are items with no movement in two years or more that are still flagged active, and 319 of them still carry a reorder point. The parameters the ERP reorders on are stale: the lead times on 67% of live items no longer match how long deliveries take, and the reorder points on 60% were never recomputed as usage and lead times changed. And receipts are posted in batches: 57% of purchase order lines carry a posting date days after the material arrived, which biases every lead time computed from them.

Two of the errors describe the same weakness in the ledger from opposite ends. Unrecorded consumption (#9) is material leaving the stockroom with no transaction, so the balance drifts above what is on the shelf until a count or a shelf-empty correction writes it down. Adjustments as a catch-all (#13) is the habit of fixing every discrepancy with an adjustment and no reason code, so those write-downs cannot be told apart from damage, receiving errors and count errors. The 4,758 chronic write-offs that evidence #9 are therefore a subset of the 10,514 unexplained adjustments counted under #13: one is the leak, the other is why the leak went unnoticed. The remaining errors are data entry. The same part is carried under two or more numbers (241 records) and the same supplier under several (7 records); costs, suppliers and reorder points are left blank (174 items); issues are posted against the wrong item (1,299), quantities keyed an order of magnitude off (512) and postings entered twice (520); and purchases are typed in under a generic code instead of the item number (2,305 lines). Each is small on its own, and together they are why a balance cannot be trusted without a count.

Two of the counts are rows that should exist rather than rows that do. For unrecorded consumption the affected rows are the write-off adjustments that stand in for the issues that were never entered; for BOM omissions they are the component rows missing from the bill of materials, counted against the complete bill.

Section 2.2

Costs

Operationally, the sixteen errors do not act one at a time. A stale lead time, an unrecorded pull and a phantom on-order balance each push the same reorder late; a duplicate record and a free-text buy each hide the same demand; a blank cost and a wrong item code each leave the same report unusable. What the shop experiences is their sum: rush orders, lines waiting for material, buyers keeping their own books, and effort spent on records that should not exist. The table below lays out those costs, how the errors produce each one, and what the 2025 record shows. Where a figure can be tied to a specific error it is given; where it cannot, the total is stated and no share of it is claimed.

Financially, the errors reach the books in four ways, and the four do not add. Some are cash spent: the freight and price premiums on rush orders. Some are cash committed early: purchases made while the same part sat on the shelf under another number. The figures above are the parts that can be traced. The larger cost is collective and does not reduce to a line item. Management ran on an inventory value, a set of product margins, a supplier scorecard and an on-order position that were all wrong at once, and every decision that leaned on them, on pricing, on purchasing, on capacity and at the year-end close, leaned on numbers that needed a physical count to believe. That, more than any single line, is the cost the go-forward changes in Section 4 are meant to end.

Section 3

Error Remediation

Remediation ran in a fixed order. We profiled every table first (row counts by year, fill rates by column, the values in each code field, the date ranges), then ran one rerunnable test for each of the sixteen error types. Where the ERP could not settle a finding on its own we brought in evidence from outside it: the cycle counts against the book balances, the purchasing manager's spreadsheet against the ERP, and the consumption expected from jobs and bills of materials against what was actually issued. Findings that rested on judgment, such as duplicate pairs, free-text matches, dead-item dispositions and BOM additions, went to the people who own them for confirmation before anything was changed, and every correction was recorded in a reference table rather than written over the source, so each one is auditable and reversible.

Section 4

Go-Forward Changes

The sixteen errors trace back to two root conditions rather than sixteen separate causes: the ERP allowed them, and nobody owned the routine upkeep that would have caught them. The corrections in Section 3 fix what those two conditions produced; the changes in this section stop them producing it again, and they fall into two groups that are different kinds of work. The first group are settings. Each was changed by the ERP administrator during the engagement, took effect for every user at once, and stops the error at the point of entry. This is the easier lift: the changes are already in place, they hold on their own, and they need nothing further from the shop. The table gives what each one does and which errors it closes (the error can no longer occur) or addresses (the error is caught or reduced, but not prevented).

The second group are habits. No setting can make someone count a shelf, review a bill of materials, close a purchase order or refresh a reorder point, so each of these needs a named owner and a cadence, and each will lapse without them. That makes this the harder lift: it depends on organizational alignment and on the owners' buy-in rather than on a configuration screen. The shop has committed to the owners and cadences below, and keeping them is what protects the results in Section 1.

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 12% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
  • What the cleanup is worth going forward is not measured here. This report claims only costs that trace to a specific error in the 2025 record. How much of the untraced rush spend and how many of the shortages clean data would have prevented is a forecast, and it belongs to the reorder-policy work that follows this audit.
