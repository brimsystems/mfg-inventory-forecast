Section 1

Results

Over ten weeks we audited the purchasing item master and the inventory ledger, corrected what could be corrected, and changed the ERP settings that let the problems recur. This section is what changed as a result, in the terms the shop runs on: whether its inventory balances can be trusted, what the errors were costing operations and the books, and what the cleanup did about each. Every figure is measured from the record. Where a cost cannot be tied to a specific error, no share of it is claimed, and what the cleanup will save going forward is left to the reorder-policy work that follows this audit rather than forecast here.

Can the balance be trusted?

This is the result the rest depends on. A balance is reliable when it was counted within the last 90 days and the count came within 5% of the system; unreliable when the item carries a fault that makes the number untrustworthy whatever the last count said (chronic unexplained write-offs, a purchase unit with no conversion to the stock unit, a duplicate record sharing one pile); and uncertain otherwise, which means no count in the last 90 days or a count 5% or more off. We applied that one rule on the day the audit began, when the shop's only count was the annual physical nine months earlier, and again at the audit date, with the remediation counts and the first quarter of the cycle-count program behind it.

On the day the audit began, no balance qualified as reliable, because the shop counted once a year and nothing had been counted in the previous 90 days; 25% of live items (370), holding 35% of the book value, were unreliable on their face. At the audit date 19% of live items (254), holding 48% of the book value, have a balance we would reorder against without a recount: 180 of the 291 A-class items and 74 of the B-class items. The unreliable share is down to 6% of items and 4% of value, 85 items whose write-off pattern has not yet had a clean quarter to clear, each named in the count schedule. The uncertain group is large by count and small by value: 608 items, nearly all C-class, have not yet had their turn in the cycle, and 365 A- and B-class items were counted but their first count found a variance of 5% or more, so they qualify at the next count if the corrected balance holds.

Beyond the ledger, the cleanup changes what can be built on the data. Forecasting, automated reordering and any model of demand consume the item master and the consumption history, and none of them could have been trusted on a master that was 40% dead, with the components on 30 of its 80 products never recorded as consumed. Management can now read an inventory value, a product margin and a supplier's real spend from the system rather than from memory, and anyone who diligences the inventory, a lender, a buyer or an auditor, finds balances with a count behind them.

One item the buyer knows. A high-volume gearmotor was carried under two part numbers, each with its own reorder point, and its supplier's real lead time had crept from about two weeks to over a month while the ERP still read two weeks. The result was recurring line stops and air freight. Merged to one record, with the lead time recomputed from receipts and the reorder point recomputed from usage over it, the item now reorders early enough to cover the true lead time, and the expedites on it stop.
Section 2

Data Quality Errors

This data quality audit examined the shop's entire ERP system end to end. The system consists of three master-level tables (Item, Supplier and Bill of Materials) and five transaction-level tables (Inventory Ledger, Purchase Orders, Service Orders, Production Orders and Cycle Counts), related as shown below.

ERP Tables

Over the past 36 months, over 227K individual records were produced across the eight ERP tables. This audit covered all of them. It found 16 different types of data quality error recur over this period. These errors touched a significant share of the ERP's records and left it unable to serve as the trusted central record of what the shop holds, buys and builds. They also directly disrupted operational workflows, inflated costs and distorted financial visibility, as detailed below.

The tables below lay out the 16 different data quality error types that were found to recur over the three-year audit period.

Three findings stand out for their scale. The item master was full of dead records: 40% of its records (980 of 2,437) were items with no movement in two years or more that are still flagged active, and 319 of them still carry a reorder point. The parameters the ERP reorders on are stale: the lead times on 67% of live items no longer match how long deliveries take, running 7 days shorter than actual on average, and the reorder points on 60% of live items differ from what current usage and lead times call for by a median of 31 units, 43% of them set too low and the rest too high. And the majority of receipts are posted in batches: 57% of purchase order lines carry a posting date 3 days on average after the material arrived, so every lead time computed from receipt dates reads 3 days longer than the delivery took, every reorder point set from it carries 3 days of stock it does not need, and every supplier measured on it looks slower than it is.

Two other master-level errors are small in the master and large downstream. The 64 components missing from the bills of 30 of the 80 products are consumed on every job that builds those products without ever being subtracted, which makes them the single largest source of shortages and write-offs in the ledger. And the 241 duplicate records, each reordering on its own point against one shared pile, produced 231 purchase lines in 2025 for parts the shop already held.

A further pattern distorts the financial view of the company: the ledger is corrected after the fact rather than kept right. Material leaves the stockroom without a transaction (#9), and the gap that opens between the books and the shelf is closed later by an adjustment carrying no reason code (#13), the same way damage, receiving errors and count errors are closed. The balance therefore reads high until someone corrects it, and the correction says nothing about why. The 4,758 chronic write-offs under #9 are part of the 10,514 unexplained adjustments under #13: one is the leak, the other is why the leak went unnoticed.

Section 3

Error Remediation

Remediation ran in a fixed order. We profiled every table first (row counts by year, fill rates by column, the values in each code field, the date ranges), then ran one rerunnable test for each of the sixteen error types. Where the ERP could not settle a finding on its own we brought in evidence from outside it: the cycle counts against the book balances, the purchasing manager's spreadsheet against the ERP, and the consumption expected from jobs and bills of materials against what was actually issued. Findings that rested on judgment, such as duplicate pairs, free-text matches, dead-item dispositions and BOM additions, went to the people who own them for confirmation before anything was changed, and every correction was recorded in a reference table rather than written over the source, so each one is auditable and reversible.

The tables below give, for each error, how it was remediated and whose input that took, the evidence it rested on, and how many of the affected rows were remediated.

Section 4

Go-Forward Changes

The sixteen errors trace back to two root conditions rather than sixteen separate causes: the ERP allowed them, and nobody owned the routine upkeep that would have caught them. The corrections in Section 3 fix what those two conditions produced; the changes in this section stop them producing it again, and they fall into two groups that are different kinds of work. The first group are settings. Each was changed by the ERP administrator during the engagement, took effect for every user at once, and stops the error at the point of entry. This is the easier lift: the changes are already in place, they hold on their own, and they need nothing further from the shop. The table gives what each one does and which errors it closes (the error can no longer occur) or addresses (the error is caught or reduced, but not prevented).

The second group are habits. No setting can make someone count a shelf, review a bill of materials, close a purchase order or refresh a reorder point, so each of these needs a named owner and a cadence, and each will lapse without them. That makes this the harder lift: it depends on organizational alignment and on the owners' buy-in rather than on a configuration screen. The shop has committed to the owners and cadences below, and keeping them is what protects the results in Section 1.

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 6% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
  • What the cleanup is worth going forward is not measured here. This report claims only costs that trace to a specific error in the 2025 record. How much of the untraced rush spend and how many of the shortages clean data would have prevented is a forecast, and it belongs to the reorder-policy work that follows this audit.
