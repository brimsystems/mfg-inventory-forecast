Section 1

Results

Over ten weeks we audited the purchasing item master and the inventory ledger, corrected what could be corrected, and changed the ERP settings that let the problems recur. This section is the outcome, in the terms the shop runs on: what the errors cost in 2025, the last full year, and what the same year looks like when the same reorder rule runs on the corrected records. Every figure is labeled measured (read from the records), simulated (from a replay of 2025 on the corrected records) or estimated (resting on an assumption, stated where it is used). The sections that follow show what we found, what we did, and what still needs an owner.

The money is in the expedites and the line stops, not in the stock. In 2025 the shop paid $163,643 in rush freight and price premiums on 536 rush lines; $53,505 of that sits on lines whose cause is in the record (a reorder placed on a stale lead time, a pull that was never recorded, an order held back for material that was never coming), and the rest were demand spikes the stale reorder points did not cover. Those shortages delayed 401 of 2,155 jobs, typically by 7 days and by 4,686 days in total. Replaying the year with the same reorder rule on the corrected records, rush spend falls to $3,627, the shortage episodes to 16 and the delayed jobs to 6. The cleanup does not release much cash from inventory, and it would be wrong to claim it does: the corrected replay holds $2,697,932 of stock on average against $2,696,281 as recorded. $550,465 of excess disappears, almost all of it duplicate records that each reordered against one shared pile, but $552,116 of shortfall is filled on items whose stale reorder points and lead times had been running them short, and the net, $1,651 more held, is inside the noise of the simulation.

Before the cleanup, only 25% of live items had a book balance we would trust to reorder against; 30% were unreliable and held 35% of the book value. After the cycle-count program and the master fixes, 62% are reliable and the unreliable share is down to 12%, each with a stated reason.

One item the buyer knows. A high-volume gearmotor was carried under two part numbers, each with its own reorder point, and its supplier's real lead time had crept from about two weeks to over a month while the ERP still read two weeks. Section 2

Findings

This audit examined one company's ERP system end to end. On the master side, the records that define what the shop buys and builds: the Item master (2,437 records), Bill of materials (747 records), Supplier master (44 records). On the transaction side, the history those masters govern: the Inventory ledger (178,732), Purchase order lines (21,513), Service order lines (9,874), Production orders (7,117), Cycle counts (4,713). That is 3 master-level components holding 3,228 records and 5 transaction-level components holding 221,949 records: 225,297 records in all across 36 months, plus the purchasing manager's spreadsheet of the 120 line-stopping components she tracks outside the system.

Section 2.2

Operational and Financial Costs

These errors cost the shop in two ways. Operationally, they turn into line stops and expedites on the components that matter, into write-offs at the annual count, and into buyers who work around the system rather than through it: in 2025 the shop placed 536 rush orders, ran short 598 times on 406 items, and carried $583,620 of on-order value that existed only on paper. Financially, the same errors misstate the balance sheet and the cost of goods sold: 35% of the book value ($814,687) sat on balances no one could trust, and material consumed without a transaction was carried as an asset until the count wrote it off. The first table gives, for each error, what it does to the operation, which line item it touches, and what that came to in 2025. Each value carries two tags. The first is the kind of money: incurred is cash the shop actually spent, and stopping it is a recurring saving; working capital is stock held above or below what the same rule would hold on corrected data, where only the net is cash; misstatement means the books were wrong and were later corrected, so no cash comes back; exposure is a wrong number that costs nothing until someone acts on it, reported with how often anyone did. The second is how the figure was reached: measured from the records, simulated by replaying 2025 with the same reorder rule on the corrected records, or estimated on a stated assumption. Not every error is a loss: a few are misattributions or timing errors that net to zero at the total and only distort where the cost sits.

Section 3

Actions

Section 3.1

Error Remediation

The remediation ran in a fixed order. We began by exploring and profiling every component of the ERP: row counts by year, fill rates by column, the distinct values in every code field and the date ranges, which surfaced the blank fields and placeholder classes before any test was written. We then ran one test for each of the sixteen error types, written as queries against the extracted tables so that each finding traces to a stated rule and can be rerun. Where the ERP could not settle a finding on its own, we brought in evidence from outside it: the physical cycle counts against the system's on-hand balances, the purchasing manager's spreadsheet against the ERP for the components she tracks, and expected consumption from jobs and bills of materials against what was actually issued, with crosswalks built for the duplicate items and the fragmented suppliers. Findings that rested on judgment rather than fact, such as duplicate pairs, free-text attributions, dead-item dispositions and BOM additions, went to the people who own them, the buyer, the stockroom lead, the engineering manager and the assembly supervisor, for confirmation before anything was changed. Lead times were recomputed with a method robust to batched receipt dates, and every correction was recorded in a reference table rather than written over the source, so each one is auditable and reversible. The tables below give, for each error, how it was remediated and whose input that took, the evidence it rested on, and how many of the affected rows were remediated.

Section 3.2

Process Changes

The sixteen errors trace back to two root conditions rather than sixteen separate causes. The first is that the ERP allowed them: it did not require a reason code on an adjustment or a cost on a new item, it offered generic item codes, it permitted a balance to go negative, it let anyone create a part, and it let the floor and receiving work under shared logins. The second is that nobody owned the routine upkeep: no one refreshed the parameters set at go-live, counted the shelves between annual physicals, closed documents, reviewed dead items or maintained the bills of materials as products changed. The corrections in Section 3.1 fix what those two conditions produced; the changes below stop them producing it again.

We separated the changes on that line because they are different kinds of work. The first group are settings. Each was changed by the ERP administrator during the engagement, took effect for every user at once on the date shown, and stops the error at the point of entry, so they are already holding and need nothing further from the shop. The second group are habits. No setting can make someone count a shelf, review a bill of materials or refresh a reorder point, so each of these needs a named owner and a cadence, and each will lapse without them. The shop has committed to the owners and cadences below; keeping them is what protects the results in Section 1.

Section 4

What remains

Not everything was resolved, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.

  • Items still unreliable. 12% of live items still carry a balance we would not trust: phantom-inventory items whose first count is scheduled in the continuing cycle-count program, each with its reason recorded.
  • Lead-time precision floor. Because receipts were batched to Mondays and month-end, computed lead times carry a few days of irreducible noise; the recommended values use a trimmed high percentile to stay safe rather than precise.
  • What the shop declined. A small set of dead items were kept active at the buyer's insistence as insurance spares, against the recommendation to deactivate them.
  • The corrected-year figures are a simulation. The 2025 replay on corrected records holds demand, suppliers and the reorder rule fixed and changes only the data the rule saw. It does not model the buyers learning the new numbers or a customer's reaction to a late job, so the expedite, shortage and inventory figures under "corrected" are the size of the effect, not a forecast of next year.
