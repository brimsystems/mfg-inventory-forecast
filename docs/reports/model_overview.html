Section 1

Summary

From January through June 2026 the shop ran its purchasing on the cleaned records and on reorder points set each month by the demand model. Against the first half of 2025, run the same way the shop had always run it, the fill rate rose from 94.2% to 97.1%, stockout episodes fell from 706 to 382, jobs held for material from 188 to 87, and rush freight and premiums from $238K to $104K. The shop holds more stock to do it: $2.40M on average against $1.91M.

Most of that improvement is the cleanup, not the model. Replaying the same six months of demand with nothing fixed gives 724 stockouts; with the cleaned records and reorder points recomputed by the simple rule, 433; with the model, 382. The model adds service on top of the cleanup, but it pays for it with inventory: once the new policy has settled (months 4 to 6), it lifts A-item fill from 97.8% to 99.8% against a 98% target, on $2.66M of stock against $2.13M. Held to the target, the simpler rule gets there more cheaply. The model's safety stock is sized for every order cycle, and the shop's large order quantities already protect most of them; sizing it against a fill-rate target instead is the next step (Section 6).

Section 3

Where the Improvement Came From

To separate what the cleanup did from what the model did, the same January to June 2026 demand, with the same supplier deliveries, was replayed three ways. With nothing fixed, the shop keeps the stale lead times and reorder points, the duplicate records, the phantom on-order and the unrecorded pulls. With the cleaned records and the recomputed rule, it runs on the corrected masters with reorder points recomputed from twelve months of usage over the corrected lead time. With the demand model, the corrected masters are the same, and the reorder point each month comes from the model's forecast. The only difference between the last two is the forecast behind the reorder point. None of the three can see future demand: the reorder decision knows scheduled production and nothing else, as the ERP does.

Section 4

Forecast Accuracy

The model forecasts each item's demand over its own replenishment lead time, because that is the quantity a reorder point has to cover. Accuracy is measured as weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) on a held-out year, which reads like the familiar percentage error for a steady part and stays defined for the third of parts with months of zero demand. The same model, with the same features, was run on the history at three stages of cleaning. Across all items the error falls from 43.9% to 37.8%; on the 95 items whose history the cleanup actually repaired (duplicates merged, unrecorded consumption restored) it falls from 66.5% to 37.4%, and on the merged duplicates alone from 71% to 38%. The gain is modest overall because only three of the sixteen errors touch the demand history; the other thirteen corrupt what the forecast is used for, which is why Section 3 is where the cleanup shows its value.

Bias is the diagnostic accuracy hides. Trained on log demand, the model forecasts something nearer the median than the mean, and ran 11% low on smooth, 18% low on erratic, 11% low on lumpy, 31% low on intermittent parts. A reorder point needs the mean, so the forecast is corrected per demand pattern by the ratio of actual to forecast on the 2025 backtest; in the six forward months the corrected forecast ran within a few percent of actual demand for every pattern.

Section 5

From Forecast to Reorder Point

At the start of each forward month the model was retrained on the cleaned history to date, and each item's reorder point was set to the corrected forecast over the lead time plus a safety stock of k times the item's forecast error over the lead time. The factor k was calibrated on the 2025 backtest as the quantile of the errors at each class's service level, rather than assumed from the normal curve: A 2.20 (normal 2.05), B 1.79 (1.64), C 1.36 (1.28). The errors have slightly heavier tails than the normal curve assumes, so the calibrated buffers are a little larger.

Buyers stop trusting numbers that jump around, so a point only moves when the fresh value differs from the current one by more than 20%. Without that band every point would change every month, and 24% of them by more than a fifth; with it, 26% of item-months see a change at all, each one large enough to be worth acting on.

Section 6

Limitations and Next Step

  • The forward window is a simulation. The six months are replayed from the generated demand and supplier behaviour, not observed. Demand, deliveries and the forecast are identical across the three variants, so the differences between them are the policy; the size of each difference is an estimate.
  • Safety stock is sized per cycle, not per unit filled. The model's buffer protects every order cycle to the class's service level, and on items with large order quantities most cycles are already protected by the order itself, so the model overshoots the A-item fill target. Setting safety stock against a fill-rate target, which accounts for order quantity, would bring inventory down toward the rule's while keeping the model's advantage on the parts that stop the line. That is the next change to make.
  • The first three months are a transition. Purchases run above consumption while stock is rebuilt; the steady-state comparison is months 4 to 6, and three months is a short steady state.
